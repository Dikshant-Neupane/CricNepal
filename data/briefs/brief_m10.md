## The one-line read

A par-plus total lost to a chase that refused to stall. Karnali made 180 —
39 runs above their own S1–S2 bat-first average and well above the TU par
band (low 150s over 20 overs, from our own 64 matches) — and still lost,
because Kathmandu's chase had two separate answers: Rashid Khan's fifty held
the middle together, and then the captain, Karan KC, finished the job himself
at more than two runs a ball. Karnali's bowling never produced the cluster
of wickets a 180-run defence needs: after the fourth wicket fell at 89 in
the 13th over, Kathmandu lost only two more wickets in the remaining 7 overs,
and the last pair still had 2 balls in hand.

## How Karnali approach games (S1–S2 evidence, tested today)

- **A bat-first total far above their own history — and it still was not
  enough.** S1–S2: batting first Karnali average 140.8 and win only 36.4%
  of those games (11 games); chasing they average 132.0 and win 60.0%.
  Today they batted first and made 180, their best batting-first total
  of the season so far, and lost. One game does not rewrite a 36.4% record,
  but it does say something sharper: at TU, a total is only as good as the
  wickets behind it, and Karnali's defence took only six wickets across
  20 overs, never breaking the chase open after the fourth wicket fell.
- **The powerplay was explosive — then the middle stalled.** Karnali were
  65/1 through the powerplay, against an S1–S2 powerplay batting average of
  39.6 runs and 1.69 wickets lost per match. Ariful Islam was the
  explosion: 44 off 22 (2.00 runs per ball against a 1.1102 prior), five
  fours and three sixes. Then came the cluster that undid the start:
  66/2 at 6.4 overs became 78/4 at 9.1, with Deepak Dumre (8 off 6, 1.33
  against a 1.1561 prior) and Mark Watt (17 off 20, 0.85 against a 1.3136
  prior — well below himself) falling within 12 runs. An explosive start
  followed by two quiet wickets is the pattern that turns 180 from a
  winning total into a chaseable one.
- **Talat and Sarraf rebuilt it properly — and it still was not a
  defence.** Hussain Talat made 40 off 30 (1.33 against a 1.1289 prior)
  before being out hit wicket, and Pawan Sarraf finished 30 not out off 24
  (1.25 against a 1.1948 prior); their fifth-wicket stand added 65 runs
  and took Karnali from 78/4 to 143/5. Gulsan Jha added 20 off 12 at the
  death, and the last three overs produced 37 runs. The batting did its
  job after the stall. The bowling did not.
- **The bowling shape: one economical bowler, and expensive everything
  else.** Mark Watt was the exception, 1/26 in 4 overs (6.50 an over
  against a 7.542 prior — cheaper than himself). Around him: Sompal Kami
  2/44 in 4 overs (11.00 against a 7.675 prior), Hussain Talat 2/32 in 4
  (8.00 against a 7.369 prior), Nandan Yadav 1/28 in 3.4 overs (7.64
  against a 7.571 prior — exactly himself), Yuvraj Khatri 0/10 in his
  single over (10.00 against a 7.462 prior) and Pawan Sarraf 0/17 in his
  single over (17.00 against a 6.98 prior). Gulsan Jha went at 11.50 in
  2 overs and has no priors row, so no versus-prior read exists for him.
  A defence that concedes 10 wide runs in the first innings and 9 in the
  second is not a disciplined defence, and tonight the wides were a
  symptom, not the cause: the cause was that nobody except Watt could
  hold an over together while Rashid and Karan were set.

## How Kathmandu approach games — and what changed tonight

- **A chase side that can chase.** S1–S2: Kathmandu chase at 130.4 on
  average and win 71.4% of their chases (7 games); batting first they
  average 128.0 and win 50.0%. Tonight they chased 181 — fifty runs above
  their S1–S2 chase average — and won. The pattern held, at a higher
  altitude: this is a side built to overhaul totals, not to set them, and
  the rest of the league now has a 181-run data point for that.
- **The powerplay was ordinary — and that was fine.** Kathmandu were
  41/2 through the powerplay, against an S1–S2 powerplay batting average
  of 38.9 runs and 2.53 wickets lost per match: about par for runs, fewer
  wickets than usual. David Warner made 16 off 10 (1.60 against a 1.2504
  prior) before Sompal Kami bowled him, and Smit Patel made 9 off 11
  before Nandan Yadav bowled him; Smit has no priors row, so no
  versus-prior read exists for him. The chase was not won in the
  powerplay. It was won after it.
- **Aadil Alam stalled it; Rashid Khan carried it.** Mohammad Aadil Alam
  made 20 off 27 (0.74 against a 1.246 prior — well below himself) and
  was out at 82/3 in the 12th over, after a 54-run third-wicket stand
  that consumed 44 balls. Rashid Khan then did the carrying: 50 off 33
  (1.52 against a 1.1761 prior), three fours and two sixes, out at 144/5
  in the 17th over after a 55-run fifth-wicket stand with the captain.
  Bhim Sharki, between them, made 5 off 4 (1.25 against a 1.1926 prior —
  exactly himself) and was out at 89/4. The shape of the chase was: one
  slow anchor, one fifty, and then the captain.
- **Karan KC won it twice.** With the ball he took 2/35 in 4 overs (8.75
  an over against a 7.818 prior — expensive, but the wickets were
  Jishan Alam and Gulsan Jha, and his 4 overs included five wide
  deliveries and the innings' only no-ball, yet he still
  finished as Player of the Match). With the bat he made 44 off 20 (2.20
  against a 1.2946 prior, and above even his 1.5506 death prior), four
  fours and three sixes, out at 160/6 in the 18th over. Jahandad Khan
  then finished it: 22 not out off 10, with Santosh Yadav 5 not out off
  3, the last pair adding 21 unbeaten runs. Jahandad has no priors row,
  so no versus-prior read exists for him; Santosh's 1.67 runs per ball
  against a 1.1728 prior is a three-ball sample and is not read as form.
- **Kathmandu's own bowling was not tidy either.** Shahab Alam took 1/31
  in 4 overs (7.75 against a 6.513 prior), Jahandad Khan 1/37 in 4 (9.25,
  no priors row), Dev Shah 1/28 in 3 overs (9.33), Ripon Mondol 0/39 in
  3 overs (13.00, no priors row) — and Santosh Yadav was the control
  spell, 1/8 in 2 overs (4.00 against a 6.926 prior). Kathmandu won
  despite conceding 180, not because they strangled Karnali: they won
  because their chase had more answers than Karnali's defence had
  questions.

## The mirror: what this means for Janakpur's own approach

- **180 is not a safety line at TU — the Bolts already know this, and
  tonight is the second proof.** Match 6 saw 186 chased by Karnali; today
  180 was chased by Kathmandu. In both games the chasing side had a
  captain or set batter still there at over 17 (Kami 62 not out in
  Match 6; Karan KC 44, then Jahandad Khan 22 not out, today). A side
  that bowls a team out, as Janakpur did to Kathmandu in Match 5 (115
  all out), does not face this problem; a side that leaves a set
  finisher in, does. The death plan is not a luxury item in this
  tournament. It is the game.
- **The captain two-way game is now a league pattern, not a one-off.**
  Karan KC tonight (44 off 20 and 2/35); Sompal Kami in Match 6 (62 not
  out and 4/33); Dipendra Singh Airee in Match 4 (51 not out); Moeen
  Ali in Match 9 (21 off 16 and 3/13). The Bolts' own version is
  Harmeet Singh's two-way role, and Tuesday's card should price the
  opposition captain the same way: as two players, not one.
- **Clusters, not totals, decide games here.** Karnali went 66/2 to 78/4
  and never recovered the initiative even though they reached 180;
  Kathmandu went 89/4 to 144/5 and won because the fifth-wicket pair
  scored 55 runs without losing another wicket for four overs. Wickets
  in clusters stall an innings; a wicketless partnership of 50-plus
  rescues one. That is the lever Janakpur's powerplay and middle-overs
  bowling has to pull on Tuesday.
